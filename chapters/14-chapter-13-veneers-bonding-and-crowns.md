# Chapter 13: Veneers, Bonding, and Crowns

The happy path is simple: protect the foundation before chasing the expensive fix. In this chapter, veneers, bonding, and crowns is treated as a practical adult decision, not a morality test.

Start with the mechanism. Teeth fail when plaque, acid, force, dryness, delayed evaluation, or irreversible drilling get ahead of your ability to control them. The useful routine is the one that interrupts those forces consistently: gentle brushing with a credible remineralizing toothpaste, interdental cleaning, water after meals, sugar-frequency control, saliva support, and risk-based professional care.

For veneers, bonding, and crowns, the best question is not “What does everyone do?” It is “What problem am I solving, what evidence supports this option, what are the tradeoffs, and what happens if it fails?” That question protects you from both neglect and sales pressure.

Seek prompt dental evaluation for swelling, fever, spreading facial pain, pus, trauma, a knocked-out or loose adult tooth, uncontrolled bleeding, severe night-waking pain, or a broken tooth with sharp edges or exposed inner structure. Routine chapters can guide habits. Symptoms that suggest infection, fracture, or active disease need a clinician.

## The Pre-Veneer Foundation Window

If you are considering veneers, the least glamorous prep is the part that protects the result: calm gums, clean margins, stable bite forces, and color planning before irreversible work begins. Porcelain margins are built at the edge where tooth meets gum. If the gums are puffy, bleeding, or changing shape, impressions or digital scans can be less predictable, and the final fit may suffer. A practical 60-day conditioning window looks like this. First, make the gumline boring: daily interdental cleaning, gentle brushing, and professional cleaning if needed. Then settle color before porcelain is made. If you plan to whiten natural teeth that will sit next to veneers, do it before final shade selection, not after. Finally, stop peroxide whitening before bonding. Many clinicians advise a waiting period because residual oxygen can interfere with resin bonding, but the exact interval depends on material, protocol, and evidence source. This is not perfectionism. It is sequencing. Cosmetic dentistry is less forgiving than a lipstick shade. Once enamel is prepared and porcelain is bonded, you are living with the consequences of planning, bite, margins, and maintenance.
