# International Oral-Health Research Standard

## Governing Principle

This book must not treat United States dental guidance, United States dental practice, American insurance conventions, or American product availability as the universal default.

Research every consequential topic from an international perspective.

The goal is to identify:

1. what the strongest available scientific evidence shows
2. where international professional guidance agrees
3. where legitimate differences exist
4. which countries achieve unusually strong oral-health outcomes
5. what those countries actually do differently
6. whether those differences plausibly explain their outcomes
7. what an individual reader can reasonably apply

American recommendations may be included, but they must be treated as one national perspective among several.

## International Evidence Sources

Prioritize sources in approximately this order:

1. systematic reviews and meta-analyses
2. international evidence-based guidelines
3. World Health Organization reports and oral-health country profiles
4. Global Burden of Disease research
5. Cochrane reviews
6. high-quality randomized controlled trials
7. national clinical guidelines from countries with strong oral-health outcomes
8. major professional organizations with transparent evidence-grading methods
9. national public-health agencies
10. strong observational, cohort, and comparative health-system research
11. mechanistic, in situ, and laboratory evidence, clearly identified as such

Do not assume that an organization is authoritative merely because it is prominent in the United States.

## Required Geographic Scope

For every major subject, search internationally.

At minimum, consider evidence and guidance from Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and the United States.

Also include relevant research from any other country that has unusually strong outcomes, influential dental research, a distinctive preventive model, long-term population data, or important evidence relevant to the claim.

Do not exclude research because it is not American. Do not include a country merely to create geographic variety. Include it when it contributes meaningful evidence, outcomes, or policy experience.

## Identifying High-Performing Countries

Do not use vague internet rankings or marketing articles titled "countries with the best teeth."

Create a reproducible comparison using the latest reasonably comparable international data.

Evaluate countries separately across metrics such as:

- prevalence of untreated caries in permanent teeth
- prevalence of untreated caries in primary teeth
- DMFT or DMFS at internationally comparable ages
- proportion of children who are caries-free
- prevalence of severe periodontal disease
- prevalence of complete tooth loss
- number of natural teeth retained in older adulthood
- oral-disease disability burden
- preventive dental-care access
- unmet need for dental treatment
- socioeconomic inequality in oral health
- affordability and public coverage
- frequency of invasive restorative intervention
- oral-cancer incidence and mortality where relevant

For each metric:

1. identify the data source
2. record the data year
3. record the population and age group
4. record the case definition
5. identify whether the result is measured, modeled, or self-reported
6. note important comparability limitations
7. identify several high-performing countries rather than forcing one overall winner

Create `research/international-outcomes-comparison.md`.

This file must explain which countries perform strongly on each metric, which data are directly comparable, which comparisons are weak or outdated, which health-system or behavioral factors may contribute, and which causal conclusions cannot be made from the available evidence.

## Outcome-Led Research

Do not begin with a preferred dental practice and then search for countries that use it.

Instead:

1. identify countries with strong outcomes
2. examine their preventive guidance, clinical practices, diet, product use, public-health programs, and access models
3. compare those practices across multiple high-performing countries
4. investigate whether supporting clinical evidence exists
5. distinguish correlation from causation

A practice used in a high-performing country is not automatically responsible for the country's results.

A practice absent from the United States is not automatically better.

A practice common in the United States is not automatically evidence-based or internationally standard.

## Required Cross-National Questions

For each major recommendation, investigate:

- Is this advice shared internationally?
- Which countries recommend it?
- Which countries do something materially different?
- Are the differences based on evidence, regulation, economics, professional culture, or historical practice?
- Do populations using the alternative approach have better, worse, or similar measurable outcomes?
- Is the recommendation intended for population health, individual high-risk patients, or clinical treatment?
- Does the recommendation depend on local water fluoridation, diet, product concentration, insurance coverage, or access to professional care?
- Is this prevention, arrest, remineralization, symptom management, or restorative treatment?
- Are different countries using different definitions or diagnostic thresholds?

## International Guideline Comparison

For consequential topics, create a comparison table in the relevant research file covering several major jurisdictions.

Topics requiring international comparison include brushing frequency, brushing duration, toothbrush type, interdental cleaning, flossing, interdental brushes, water flossers, fluoride toothpaste, nano-hydroxyapatite toothpaste, toothpaste concentration, rinsing after brushing, mouthwash, professional cleaning frequency, dental examination frequency, radiograph frequency, caries-risk assessment, early caries management, sealants, silver diamine fluoride, resin infiltration, periodontal screening, scaling and root planing, wisdom-tooth removal, root-canal treatment, crowns, replacement of existing fillings, orthodontic treatment, occlusion and bite claims, bruxism and night guards, whitening, diet and sugar frequency, acidic drinks, xylitol, and other sugar alcohols.

Record meaningful disagreements rather than averaging them into generic advice.

## Product and Ingredient Research

Evaluate dental ingredients internationally.

For each ingredient or product category, investigate active concentration, formulation, delivery method, contact time, frequency of use, studied population, comparator, clinical endpoint, duration of study, adverse effects, regulatory status, availability by country, and whether evidence is clinical, in situ, laboratory, or mechanistic.

Do not evaluate an ingredient solely by whether it is currently accepted by the American Dental Association.

Do not evaluate it solely by whether it is commercially popular in Japan, Europe, or another region.

Evaluate the full evidence.

## Nano-Hydroxyapatite Requirements

Nano-hydroxyapatite must receive a complete international evidence review.

Include Japanese research and usage history, European research, randomized clinical trials, systematic reviews, comparisons with fluoride toothpaste, concentration differences, formulation differences, remineralization endpoints, caries-prevention endpoints, dentin-hypersensitivity endpoints, white-spot lesion research, in situ and laboratory findings, safety and particle-characterization questions, limitations of the evidence, and regulatory and professional-guideline differences.

Do not dismiss nano-hydroxyapatite because it lacks a particular American endorsement.

Do not claim universal superiority over fluoride unless the comparative clinical evidence supports that exact conclusion.

Distinguish carefully among evidence of remineralization, evidence of reduced sensitivity, evidence of caries prevention, noninferiority to fluoride, superiority to fluoride, and combination formulations.

When evidence differs by outcome, say so explicitly.

## Source-Language Policy

Search for relevant research published in languages other than English.

When reliable English translations, English abstracts, or bilingual official documents exist, use them.

Do not claim to have fully evaluated a non-English document unless enough translated content was available to understand its methodology and conclusions.

Record translation limitations.

## Avoiding Institutional Bias

Never use any of the following as sufficient reasoning:

- "The ADA recommends it."
- "Dentists generally agree."
- "This is the standard of care."
- "It is not approved in the United States."
- "It has been used for decades."
- "It is natural."
- "It is popular in Japan."
- "European dentists use it."
- "A manufacturer says it works."

Each may be relevant context. None independently establishes that a claim is true.

## Conflict Resolution

When international guidelines disagree:

1. describe the disagreement accurately
2. compare the evidence each guideline cites
3. check the publication dates
4. examine whether the populations or health systems differ
5. identify relevant conflicts of interest
6. distinguish evidence-based disagreement from local practice convention
7. state which interpretation appears best supported
8. preserve genuine uncertainty

Do not automatically defer to the most conservative guidance.

Do not automatically prefer the newest guidance.

Do not automatically prefer guidance that aligns with the author's initial view.

## Citation Verification

Every consequential scientific or medical citation must be checked against the original source before inclusion.

For every cited source, verify title, authors or issuing organization, publication, year, study design, population, intervention, comparator, outcome, conclusion, major limitations, and DOI, PMID, or stable official URL where available.

Never cite a source based only on another article's description of it.

Never invent a citation.

Never cite a search-result snippet as evidence.

When the original source cannot be retrieved, mark `[PRIMARY SOURCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED]`.
