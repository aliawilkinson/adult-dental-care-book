# Claims and Evidence Policy

## International Research Standard

This policy works together with `international-research-standard.md`.

Do not treat United States dental guidance, United States product availability, American insurance conventions, or American regulatory status as the universal default. American recommendations may be included, but they are one national perspective among several.

The governing research frame is:

> Compare international evidence, guidelines, population outcomes, and clinical practices, prioritizing high-quality evidence and countries that demonstrably perform well on the relevant outcome.

## Evidence Priority

Prefer, in this order:

1. systematic reviews and meta-analyses
2. international evidence-based guidelines
3. World Health Organization reports and oral-health country profiles
4. Global Burden of Disease research
5. Cochrane reviews
6. high-quality randomized controlled trials
7. national clinical guidelines from countries with strong oral-health outcomes
8. major professional organizations with transparent evidence-grading methods
9. national public-health agencies
10. strong observational, cohort, and comparative health-system research
11. mechanistic, in situ, and laboratory evidence, clearly identified as such
12. expert clinical reasoning, clearly labeled
13. manufacturer claims only when independently substantiated

## Research Rules

- Prefer original studies, international evidence syntheses, official professional guidance, and comparable population outcome data over secondary summaries.
- Record the exact source for consequential claims.
- Do not infer that a product works clinically merely because its mechanism is plausible.
- Distinguish prevention, remineralization, symptom relief, and reversal of established disease.
- Distinguish evidence in vitro, in situ, and in humans.
- State meaningful limitations.
- Represent fluoride and nano-hydroxyapatite evidence accurately rather than ideologically.
- Evaluate concentrations, formulations, and endpoints, not merely ingredient names.
- Do not treat absence from one American guideline as proof of ineffectiveness.
- Do not treat international use as proof of superiority.
- Do not invent equivalence or superiority conclusions.
- Do not begin with a preferred practice and then search for countries that use it. Identify outcomes first, then examine practices and evidence.
- Do not rely on vague rankings of "countries with the best teeth."
- Record jurisdiction, population, year, case definition, and comparability limits for international outcome claims.
- Record meaningful disagreement among international guidelines instead of averaging it into generic advice.
- Search for relevant non-English research when appropriate, using reliable translations, English abstracts, or bilingual official documents where available.
- Flag claims that require author review.

## Citation Rules

- Never fabricate citations, quotation text, statistics, DOIs, organizations, or guideline statements.
- Cite consequential factual, scientific, medical, historical, regulatory, product, and safety claims.
- Source the fluoride/nHA chapter especially carefully, including mechanisms, toothpaste history, water fluoridation, risk claims, regulatory differences, and comparative evidence.
- If a claim needs support and no reliable support has been found, write `[SOURCE NEEDED: precise claim]`.
- If the original source cannot be retrieved, write `[PRIMARY SOURCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED]`.
- If a recommendation depends on jurisdiction, product formulation, age, pregnancy, medical condition, or existing dental work, say so.
- If a claim is based on the author's routine or preference, label it as such.

## Reader-Facing Citation Balance

Keep citations honest but not oppressive. The manuscript should be readable first. Put detailed evidence trails in `research/evidence-ledger.md`, `research/claim-map.md`, and chapter source notes.

## Institutional Bias Checks

Never use any of the following as sufficient reasoning:

- "The ADA recommends it."
- "Dentists generally agree."
- "This is the standard of care."
- "It is not approved in the United States."
- "It has been used for decades."
- "It is natural."
- "It is popular in Japan."
- "European dentists use it."
- "A manufacturer says it works."

Each may be relevant context. None independently establishes that a claim is true.

## Safety Rules

Escalate to dental or medical evaluation for:

- swelling, fever, spreading facial pain, or trouble breathing or swallowing
- severe tooth pain
- trauma, knocked-out teeth, or major chips
- persistent bleeding or gum swelling
- a mouth sore that does not heal within 2 weeks
- lingering sensitivity, especially one-sided or worsening sensitivity
- visible cracks, loose restorations, or signs of infection

Do not diagnose the reader. Explain possibilities, urgency, and what type of professional evaluation is appropriate.
