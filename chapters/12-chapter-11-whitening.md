# Chapter 11: Whitening

The happy path is simple: protect the foundation before chasing the expensive fix. In this chapter, whitening is treated as a practical adult decision, not a morality test.

Start with the mechanism. Teeth fail when plaque, acid, force, dryness, delayed evaluation, or irreversible drilling get ahead of your ability to control them. The useful routine is the one that interrupts those forces consistently: gentle brushing with a credible remineralizing toothpaste, interdental cleaning, water after meals, sugar-frequency control, saliva support, and risk-based professional care.

For whitening, the best question is not “What does everyone do?” It is “What problem am I solving, what evidence supports this option, what are the tradeoffs, and what happens if it fails?” That question protects you from both neglect and sales pressure.

Seek prompt dental evaluation for swelling, fever, spreading facial pain, pus, trauma, a knocked-out or loose adult tooth, uncontrolled bleeding, severe night-waking pain, or a broken tooth with sharp edges or exposed inner structure. Routine chapters can guide habits. Symptoms that suggest infection, fracture, or active disease need a clinician.

## A Sensible Whitening Cycle

Whitening is chemistry, not housekeeping. Carbamide peroxide breaks down into hydrogen peroxide, which oxidizes stain molecules inside enamel and dentin. That is why it can change tooth color in a way whitening toothpaste cannot. It is also why sensitivity and gum irritation are the watch-points.

A conservative adult protocol to discuss with a dentist is 10% carbamide peroxide in custom trays, used for a short active cycle and then occasional maintenance. The author's preferred scaffold protocol is seven nights for an initial cycle, then maintenance rather than constant whitening. The exact wear time, whether overnight is appropriate, and whether you should whiten at all depend on your teeth, restorations, gum recession, sensitivity history, and whether there is untreated decay. Do not whiten over mystery pain, active cavities, cracked teeth, heavy tartar, or inflamed gums. You may get more pain, patchier results, and a very boring lesson in why sequence matters. Existing crowns, veneers, bonding, and fillings will not whiten with peroxide, so whitening before matching new cosmetic work is often the cleaner sequence.

After whitening, a gentle remineralizing toothpaste can be useful for comfort, but do not claim that it “instantly seals” all tubules or eliminates sensitivity unless a specific product and protocol has evidence. The honest version is better: peroxide can increase sensitivity for some people, and remineralizing or desensitizing products may help manage it.
