# Chapter 2: How Teeth Stay Healthy

Your mouth is in a mineral negotiation all day. Acids pull calcium and phosphate out of enamel. Saliva brings minerals back, buffers acid, lubricates tissue, and helps wash away food. Demineralization is mineral loss. Remineralization is mineral return. The goal is not to create a sterile mouth. The goal is to keep the chemistry tilted toward repair more often than damage.

Plaque matters because it holds bacteria against the tooth surface. When bacteria metabolize fermentable carbohydrates, they produce acids. When the pH at the tooth surface drops low enough, enamel begins losing mineral. Frequent snacking or sipping keeps the mouth in repeated acid episodes, even if each individual exposure seems small.

This is why timing matters. A dessert eaten with a meal is usually less hostile than sugar grazed for hours. Coffee, wine, citrus, sports drinks, soda, and many flavored waters can soften enamel temporarily. Rinsing with water, chewing sugar-free gum, and waiting before brushing are not moral victories. They are chemistry management.

## The Mineral Cycle You Are Trying To Influence

A useful dental routine is not magic. It is an attempt to make the demineralization and remineralization cycle less chaotic. When acids sit against enamel, mineral leaves the tooth surface. When the acid clears, saliva helps neutralize the mouth and brings calcium and phosphate back toward the enamel surface. Many dental texts describe enamel's critical pH near 5.5, though the exact threshold depends on saliva chemistry, plaque fluid, fluoride exposure, and the mineral saturation around the tooth. This is why the routine in this book keeps returning to timing. You are not only cleaning debris. You are managing the hours when the tooth surface is softened, dry, or mineral-hungry. Water after coffee is not glamorous. Neither is leaving toothpaste residue on your teeth at night. Both make sense because they shorten or improve the chemical recovery window.

Nano-hydroxyapatite belongs in this conversation because hydroxyapatite is the mineral family that makes enamel hard. A good nHA product is trying to deliver tiny mineral particles that can interact with microscopic surface defects and exposed dentin tubules. That does not mean every nHA product has proven the same clinical outcome. Remineralization, sensitivity reduction, white-spot improvement, and prevention of future cavities are related but not identical claims. The dedicated fluoride and nHA chapter separates those outcomes instead of tossing them into one shiny marketing bucket.
